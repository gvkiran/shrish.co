What Is Kaja? The Crispy, Syrup-Soaked Sweet of Coastal Andhra

Kaja (also spelled khaja) is a deep-fried, layered Indian sweet — crisp and flaky on the outside, then soaked in sugar syrup so the inside turns soft and juicy. In coastal Andhra Pradesh it is one of the most loved festival and gifting sweets, made in two celebrated styles: the hollow, tube-shaped Gottam Kaja and the folded, many-layered Madatha Kaja.

If you have seen a golden, crisp, syrup-glazed sweet at an Andhra festival or wedding and wondered what it is, this guide explains what kaja is, the difference between its two famous types, and where to buy the authentic version.

Kaja or khaja — what is the name?

The sweet is known as khaja across much of India and simply kaja in Telugu-speaking Andhra Pradesh. It descends from an old tradition of layered, fried pastry, but the coastal-Andhra version has become its own distinct, syrup-soaked sweet with a devoted following.

Where kaja comes from — Kakinada and Tapeswaram

Two towns in the Godavari delta of Andhra Pradesh are most famous for kaja. Kakinada Kaja dates back to 1891, when Chittipeddi Kotaiah began making it in the coastal town of Kakinada; the recipe has been carried down through his family for generations. Nearby Tapeswaram is renowned for its Madatha Kaja. Kaja is such a point of regional pride that India Post issued a special stamp honouring Kakinada Kaja in 2022, and efforts have been underway since 2018 to secure a Geographical Indication (GI) tag that would formally tie authentic Kakinada Kaja to its home region.

Gottam Kaja vs Madatha Kaja

The two classic styles look and eat quite differently:

  • Gottam Kaja: “gottam” means a hollow tube or reed. The dough is shaped into a hollow, cylindrical stick, fried until shatteringly crisp, then soaked in syrup — light, airy, and crunchy.
  • Madatha Kaja: “madata” means a fold. The dough is folded into dozens of fine layers, deep-fried until golden, and soaked in syrup, so it is crisp on the outside with soft, juicy layers inside. The Tapeswaram Madatha Kaja is the benchmark.

How kaja is made

Kaja starts with a refined-wheat-flour (maida) dough that is rested and worked with ghee to build layers. It is then shaped — rolled into hollow tubes for gottam kaja, or folded into many thin layers for madatha kaja — and deep-fried slowly until crisp and golden. Finally it is dipped in a cardamom-scented sugar syrup. The skill lies in getting the layers crisp enough to stay light, yet able to drink in the syrup without turning soggy.

How to eat and store kaja

Kaja is ready to eat straight from the box — no preparation needed. Because it is syrup-soaked, it is best enjoyed fresh; keep it in an airtight container in a cool, dry place and finish it within its shelf life for the best crisp-yet-juicy texture.

Kaja vs putharekulu vs laddu

These three Andhra favourites are easy to mix up but very different. Kaja is the crisp, deep-fried, syrup-soaked sweet described here. Putharekulu is the paper-thin, wrapped sweet from Atreyapuram. Laddu is the round, bound sweet ball. Many families order a mix of all three for festivals and celebrations.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is kaja the same as khaja?

Yes. Khaja is the name used across much of India; kaja is the Telugu name for the coastal-Andhra style of the same syrup-soaked, layered fried sweet.

What is the difference between gottam kaja and madatha kaja?

Gottam kaja is shaped into a hollow, crisp tube; madatha kaja is folded into many fine layers that stay soft and juicy inside. Both are soaked in sugar syrup.

What is kaja made of?

A refined-wheat-flour (maida) dough worked with ghee, deep-fried until crisp, then soaked in a cardamom-scented sugar syrup.

How long does kaja stay fresh?

Because it is syrup-soaked, kaja is best enjoyed fresh. Stored airtight in a cool, dry place, it keeps for the shelf life printed on the pack.
